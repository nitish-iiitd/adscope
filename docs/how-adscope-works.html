How AdScope Works

A plain-English guide to a tool that finds where your advertising belongs — by predicting what your audience asks AI assistants, and where those assistants send the people who ask.

For any audience — no technical knowledge needed End-to-end walkthrough Ready to present

AdScope tells you where your advertising should appear — which websites, YouTube channels and apps — by predicting the questions your target audience asks AI assistants, and then discovering where those assistants send the people who ask them.

There is a live version inside the app, under How it works in the top navigation. That page renders the actual instructions AdScope sends to the AI models, pulled from the running system, so it can never drift out of date. This document is the portable version, for sharing outside the tool.

The shift

Why this exists

For years, deciding where to advertise meant relying on a planner's experience, traffic reports, and audience panels. That still matters — but how people discover products has changed.

Increasingly, people don't start with a search engine or a magazine. They ask an AI assistant — ChatGPT, Gemini, and others — questions like "What's a good organic sunscreen for daily use in India?" The assistant answers by drawing on a set of trusted websites, and often names them.

That creates a new, high-value question for advertisers: when my audience asks an AI assistant about my product category, which publishers does that assistant lean on — and where is that audience's attention actually sitting? Those places shape the customer's decision at the moment of intent — and AdScope finds them systematically instead of guessing.

The idea

A simple thought experiment, run at scale

AdScope does three things, in order:

Imagine
Picture your customer talking to an AI assistant. What would they actually type?
Ask
Put those same questions to AI assistants and note where they'd point — the websites they'd consult, and the channels and apps that audience uses.
Rank
Collect everything and rank it. What keeps appearing is where your brand most wants to be seen.

That's the whole concept. Everything else is about doing this reliably, transparently, and with a human in control.

01

You describe the campaign

Fill in a short form: client, campaign, target country, objective, an optional budget, and a free-text briefing describing the product, audience, and positioning. You also make two choices that shape what comes back — which publisher types to look for (websites, YouTube channels, applications, or any combination), and whether to exclude competitors.

Example brief A premium organic skincare brand targeting women aged 25–40 in major Indian cities. Objective: brand awareness.
02

AI drafts the questions your audience would ask

AdScope sends the brief to several AI models at once and asks each: "What would a real person in this audience type into their own AI assistant?" Each returns a set of realistic questions — about 30 in total across three models, covering research, comparison, buying, how-to, and local angles. A progress bar shows how many models have answered while you wait.

Example questions "What are the best premium organic skincare brands in India right now?" · "Which face serums work well for humid monsoon weather?" · "Where do beauty editors recommend shopping for skincare online?"
03

You review and refine the questions Human step

AdScope pauses and shows you every drafted question in a clean editor. You can edit the wording, remove ones that don't fit, deselect some to keep on record, or add your own. Nothing proceeds until you submit — the AI proposes, you decide.

04

AI finds the publishers for each question

For every question you approved, AdScope asks each AI model, once for each publisher type you ticked. Each returns a scored list with a short reason per entry. Ten approved questions × three models × two types is sixty separate AI calls — run in the background behind a live progress bar that counts the answers as they arrive.

05

AdScope ranks everything into one list

All those lists are merged into a single ranked list per publisher type — what you present and act on. How the ranking works is next.

The controls

What each choice on the form actually changes

Not every field does the same kind of work. Some steer which questions get drafted, others steer which publishers come back. Being precise about this saves a lot of misplaced expectation.

FieldWhere it actsWhat it does
Client briefingStage 2Does most of the work. A brief naming the audience, category, positioning and season produces far sharper questions than a vague one.
Client nameStages 2 & 4Part of the brief. Models are told not to mention your brand in the questions — a customer asks about a need, not about you. Also named in the competitor rule.
Target countryStage 2Shapes the market the questions are written for, which is what pulls local publishers into the answers.
ObjectiveStage 2Tilts the kind of question drafted. Awareness pulls toward broad discovery questions, surfacing large editorial publishers; consideration or performance pulls toward comparison and buying questions, surfacing review sites and marketplaces.
BudgetStage 2Context only. It can nudge the models toward premium or mass-market framing. It does not filter publishers by cost.
Publisher typesStage 4Decides which discovery questions get asked and which result tabs you get. Each extra type multiplies the number of AI calls.
Exclude competitorsStage 4Adds a rule to every discovery instruction and filters the results afterwards.
Queries per modelStage 2How many questions each model drafts (default 10).
Results per queryStage 4How many publishers each model may return per question — set separately per type (default 10 each).
Final list sizeStage 5How long the finished ranking may be, per publisher type (default 50). A trim of the bottom only.

Objective and budget influence the questions, not the ranking. This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to assume otherwise. Both are read only when the audience questions are drafted. The discovery stage sees just one approved question at a time — it is never shown the objective or the budget, and no score is weighted by either. Their influence is real but entirely indirect: different questions in, different publishers out. If you want the objective to bite harder, say so in the briefing and keep the matching questions at the review step.

Reliability

Why several AI models, not one

Any single AI model can be biased or incomplete. AdScope deliberately asks multiple independent models and looks for agreement — the same principle as consulting several experts and trusting what they concur on.

If three models all name a publisher, that's a strong signal. If only one does, it's weaker — worth noting, not over-weighting. AdScope is also extensible: it isn't locked to a fixed set of models. New AI providers can be added as they emerge, without redesigning the system.

The ranking

Two simple ideas decide the order

No maths required to explain either one.

Idea 1 — Agreement

For a single question, if several models independently name the same publisher, they agree. Their scores are averaged into one entry. More agreement means more confidence it is genuinely relevant to that question.

Idea 2 — Breadth

A publisher that appears for many of your audience's questions is broadly important — it sits at the centre of the conversation, not just one corner of it.

Publishers are sorted by, in strict order: breadth (how many of your questions surfaced it — a publisher appearing for eight questions outranks one appearing for two, whatever their scores), then final score to break ties, then name so the order is stable rather than arbitrary. In plain terms: a publisher that keeps appearing across many customer questions, that several AI models agree on, ranks at the top. Every row carries both signals so you can see exactly why it ranked where it did:

Queries
How many of your questions surfaced it (breadth).
Models
The most AI models that agreed on it within a single question (agreement).
Breadth
A High / Medium / Low badge. High means it appeared for at least 66% of your approved questions, Medium at least 33%, Low below that.
Score
An overall relevance score from 0 to 100 — the model's own judgement of relevance, never traffic, reach or price.

Each publisher type is ranked separately, so a website never competes for position with an app.

Publisher types

Websites, YouTube channels and apps

These are not three flavours of the same search. Each is asked a genuinely different question, because the underlying behaviour differs — an AI assistant doesn't cite a mobile app to answer a factual question, so asking it that way would produce nonsense.

TypeThe question askedHow duplicates are matched
Websites"To answer this, which websites or publishers would you consult or cite?" — a citation frameBy domain, ignoring https://, www. and any path.
YouTube channels"Whose content is a person with this need genuinely likely to watch?" — an attention frameBy channel handle, so a full URL and a bare @handle collapse into one.
Applications"Which apps is a person with this need likely to have installed and use?" — an attention frameBy app name. Models return different store listings for iOS, Android and different regions, so the name is the only stable identity; platform is kept as a detail.

Everything after that point — agreement, breadth, ranking, the final trim — is identical across the three.

Competitors

Keeping rivals out of a buying list

The output is a buying list. A rival brand will not sell you space on its own website, channel or app, so a competitor in your results isn't just awkward — it is a row nobody can act on.

Ticking exclude competitors on the form handles this in two passes, because one is not reliable on its own:

Instruct
A rule is added to every discovery instruction, naming your brand and any competitors you list.
Filter
AI models follow negative instructions unevenly, so anything that still comes back matching a competitor you named is removed before the ranking is built. This pass doesn't depend on a model's cooperation.

The filter matches on brand identity rather than exact spelling, so naming Kama Ayurveda also catches Kama Ayurveda India and kamaayurveda.com. Naming competitors is optional but makes a large difference: with names the filter is exact; without them you are relying on the models' own read of who competes with you. Leaving the box unticked keeps everything, which is right when you are researching a category rather than planning a buy.

A retailer that stocks a rival is not a competitor. The rule targets brand-owned properties only. Marketplaces, retailers, magazines and creators that cover many brands are exactly where you want to advertise, even though they carry competitors' products — so they are deliberately kept. Name only the brands you compete with, not the places that sell them.

What kind of publisher it is — e.g. Fashion & Lifestyle.

The whole list exports to CSV for spreadsheets, decks, or sharing — and every recommendation is fully traceable. No black box.

A practical way to read the table: treat the High-breadth rows as your core buy, Medium as the expansion list, and Low as leads worth a look rather than conclusions.

Worked example

The skincare campaign, all the way through

  1. Brief. Premium organic skincare, women 25–40, Indian metros, awareness. Publisher types: websites and YouTube channels. Competitors excluded, naming Nykaa and Purplle.
  2. Drafted questions. ~30 across three models — from "Best organic skincare for women in their 30s in India?" to "Which websites review clean beauty brands?"
  3. Your review. You keep the 12 sharpest, tweak two, and add one of your own: "Which sites review vegan skincare?"
  4. Discovery. 13 questions × 3 models × 2 types = 78 AI calls, run in the background behind a live progress bar.
  5. Final ranking. For "Which face serums work in humid monsoon weather?", two of three models name vogue.in at 88 and 82 — one entry at 85, named by 2 models. Across all 13 questions it comes back for 11, averaging 84, so it tops the list with a High breadth badge. Nykaa would have ranked near the top too, but it was named as a competitor and never appears. A niche blog that scored a brilliant 95 on one question and never reappeared sits far below — one strong opinion, no corroboration.

The output is defensible: for any row, you can show the exact questions and models behind it.

Flexibility

Tuned to the situation, no rebuild needed

A few dials adjust how much ground AdScope covers:

Questions
How many questions each model drafts (default 10).
Results per question
How many publishers each model returns per question — set separately for websites, YouTube channels and apps (default 10 each).
List size
How long the final list can be, per publisher type (default 50).
Publisher types
Which of the three kinds to look for. Each one added multiplies the AI calls.

These can be set per campaign on the form, or globally in configuration. Bigger numbers mean broader coverage but longer runs — a simple speed-versus-thoroughness dial. A built-in Demo Mode runs the entire flow with realistic sample data and no live AI calls — ideal for demonstrations and training without incurring cost.

Trust

Advisory and auditable, by design

Human-in-the-loop. The system never runs end to end without your review of the questions. You steer what it investigates.

Full traceability. Every recommendation traces back to the specific questions and models that produced it. The in-app How it works page even shows the exact instructions sent to the models.

Resilient. If one model fails or is slow, the others still produce results; a run only fails if everything fails, and partial gaps are flagged as "completed with warnings."

Honest about its nature. Recommendations are AI-generated and advisory. Suitability, audience data, pricing, availability, and brand safety must be verified by a qualified professional before any spend. AdScope does not invent traffic numbers or pricing.

What it is not

A measurement. It reflects what these models said at the time you ran it — not a guarantee of citation, traffic, or placement availability.

A source of commercial data. No inventory, no rate cards, no reach figures. The models are explicitly instructed never to invent them.

A brand-safety check. A publisher can be highly relevant and entirely wrong for your brand. That judgement stays human.

Unbounded by your questions. It can only find publishers relevant to the questions you approved — which is exactly why that step is yours.

Think of AdScope as a very well-read research assistant that rapidly surfaces where the conversation is happening — with a human expert always making the final call.
