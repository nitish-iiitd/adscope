1. The idea in one minute

{{ app_name }} tells you which websites, YouTube channels and apps your advertising should appear on — by predicting the questions your target audience asks AI assistants, then discovering where those assistants send people who ask them.

For decades, deciding where to advertise meant a planner's experience, traffic reports and audience panels. That still matters. But a growing share of buying journeys now starts with someone typing a question into ChatGPT or Gemini rather than a search box or a magazine. The assistant answers by leaning on a handful of sources it trusts — and frequently names them.

That creates a question worth money to an advertiser:

When my audience asks an AI assistant about my product category, which publishers does that assistant lean on — and where is that audience's attention actually sitting?

{{ app_name }} answers it by running the obvious experiment at scale: imagine your customer talking to an assistant, ask real AI models those same questions, and collect where they point. The names that keep coming back — across many customer questions, and across several independent models — are the ones worth buying.

Why several AI models, not one?
Any single model can be biased, patchy or simply wrong. {{ app_name }} asks {{ provider_count }} independent models the same thing and looks for agreement — the same instinct as asking several experts and trusting what they concur on. One model naming a publisher is a hint; all of them naming it is a signal. Models can be added or swapped without redesigning anything.

2. The five stages, end to end

  1. 1

    You describe the campaign You

    A short form: client, campaign name, target country, objective, optional budget, and a free-text briefing covering product, audience and positioning. You also pick which kinds of publisher to look for, and whether competitors should be excluded.

  2. 2

    The models draft your audience's questions AI

    Your brief goes to every model at once with one instruction: imagine the real people in this audience — what would they type into their own AI assistant? Each model returns a set of realistic questions, deliberately varied across research, comparison, how-to, buying and local intent. With {{ provider_count }} models at the default of {{ defaults.queries_per_provider }} each, that is a pool of about {{ provider_count * defaults.queries_per_provider }} drafts.

  3. 3

    You review and refine You

    {{ app_name }} stops here and waits. Every drafted question appears in an editor where you can reword, delete, deselect or add your own. Nothing proceeds until you submit. This is the deliberate human gate: the AI proposes the questions, you decide which ones are worth spending compute on.

  4. 4

    The models say where they'd send that audience AI

    Every question you approved goes to every model, once for each publisher type you ticked. Each answer is a scored list with a short reason per entry. This is the long stage — it is one AI call per question × model × type — and the page shows a live progress bar while it runs.

  5. 5

    Everything merges into one ranked list Result

    All those lists collapse into a single ranking per publisher type, with the evidence attached to every row: which of your questions surfaced it and which models named it. Export to CSV when you're happy.

3. What each field on the form actually changes

Not every field does the same kind of work. Some steer which questions get drafted, others steer which publishers come back, and a couple only change how much the system explores. This is the honest breakdown.

FieldWhere it actsWhat it does
Client briefing Stage 2 Does most of the work. The models read it to picture your audience, so a brief naming the audience, category, positioning and season produces sharper questions than a vague one.
Client name Stage 2 Stage 4 Part of the brief. The models are told not to mention your brand in the drafted questions — a customer asks about a need, not about you. It is also named in the competitor rule if you switch exclusion on.
Target country Stage 2 Shapes the market the questions are written for, which is what pulls local publishers into the answers downstream.
Objective Stage 2 Tilts the kind of question drafted. An awareness objective pulls the pool toward broad discovery and category questions, which tends to surface large editorial publishers. A consideration or performance objective pulls toward comparison and buying questions, which surfaces review sites, marketplaces and specialists. It does not change any score.
Budget Stage 2 Context only, and the weakest lever here. It sits in the brief and can nudge the models toward premium or mass-market framing. It does not filter publishers by cost, and {{ app_name }} never invents rate cards — nothing in the output is priced.
Publisher types Stage 4 Decides which discovery questions get asked, and therefore which result tabs you get. Each type is asked in its own way (see section 7). Every extra type multiplies the number of AI calls.
Exclude competitors Stage 4 Adds a rule to every discovery prompt and filters the results afterwards. See section 6.
Queries per model Stage 2 How many questions each model drafts (default {{ defaults.queries_per_provider }}). Raises the size of the pool you review.
Results per query Stage 4 How many publishers each model may return per question, set separately per type (defaults: {{ defaults.max_websites_per_query }} websites, {{ defaults.max_youtube_per_query }} channels, {{ defaults.max_apps_per_query }} apps). Higher means a longer tail and more thinly-evidenced entries.
Final list size Stage 5 How long the finished ranking may be, per publisher type (default {{ defaults.max_final_websites }}). Purely a trim of the bottom — it changes nothing above the cut.
Objective and budget influence the questions, not the ranking
This is worth being precise about, because it is easy to assume otherwise. Objective and budget are read only when the audience questions are drafted. The discovery stage sees just one approved question at a time — it is never shown the objective or the budget, and no score is weighted by either. Their influence is real but entirely indirect: different questions in, different publishers out. If you want the objective to bite harder, say so in the briefing and keep the questions that match it at the review step.

5. How the scoring works

Three things happen to a publisher between a model naming it and it appearing in your ranking. None of them involve maths you need a calculator for.

Step 1

Each model scores what it names

Every publisher a model returns comes with a 0–100 relevance score and a one-line reason. That number is the model's own judgement of how relevant and authoritative that publisher is for that one question — it is not traffic, reach, or price, and the models are explicitly instructed never to invent those figures.

Step 2

Agreement: the models are compared

For each question, the models' lists are matched up. The same publisher named by several models is merged into one entry, its score becomes the average of what those models gave it, and it records how many models named it. Matching is by identity, not spelling: www.Vogue.in/beauty and vogue.in are one publisher.

Step 3

Breadth: the questions are compared

Then the per-question lists merge into one. A publisher's breadth is how many of your approved questions surfaced it at all, and its final score is the average of its per-question scores. Breadth is the strongest signal in the system.

The ranking rule

Publishers are sorted by, in strict order:

  1. Breadth — how many of your questions surfaced it. A publisher appearing for eight of your questions outranks one appearing for two, whatever their scores.
  2. Final score — the average relevance across those questions, used to break ties within the same breadth.
  3. Name — alphabetical, so the order is stable rather than arbitrary.

Breadth leads on purpose. A high score on a single narrow question means a publisher is good at one thing; showing up again and again across the whole spread of your audience's questions means it sits at the centre of the conversation. That is the one you want your brand next to.

The breadth badge

The High / Medium / Low badge in the results is that same breadth as a share of your approved questions:

BadgeMeaningExample with 10 approved questions
HighAppeared for at least {{ breadth_high_pct }}% of your questions7 or more
MediumAppeared for at least {{ breadth_medium_pct }}%4 to 6
LowAppeared for fewer than that1 to 3

A worked example

Say you approved 10 questions and three models are running. For the question "{{ example_query }}", two of the three models name vogue.in, scoring it 88 and 82 — so for that question it is one entry at 85, named by 2 models. Repeat for the other nine questions: vogue.in comes back for eight of the ten, averaging 84. It lands near the top with breadth 8, a High badge, and a final score of 84. A niche blog that scored a brilliant 95 on one question and never reappeared sits far below it — one strong opinion, no corroboration.

If a model fails, the run continues
Models time out and rate-limit. A failed call is recorded and the ranking is simply built from the models that did answer — the campaign is marked completed with warnings so you know the consensus was thinner than usual. Only a total failure fails the campaign.

6. Keeping competitors out

This list is a buying list. A rival brand will not sell you space on its own website, channel or app, so a competitor sitting in your results isn't just awkward — it is a row nobody can act on. Ticking exclude competitors on the form deals with that in two passes, because one is not reliable on its own.

  1. The models are told. The rule shown in section 4 is appended to every discovery prompt, naming your brand and any competitors you list.
  2. The results are filtered anyway. AI models follow negative instructions unevenly, so anything that still comes back matching a competitor you named is removed before the ranking is built. This pass is deterministic — it does not depend on a model's cooperation.

The filter matches on brand identity rather than exact spelling, so naming Kama Ayurveda also catches Kama Ayurveda India and kamaayurveda.com.

A retailer that stocks a rival is not a competitor
The rule targets brand-owned properties only. Marketplaces, retailers, magazines and creators that cover many brands are exactly where you want to advertise, even though they carry competitors' products — so they are deliberately kept. Name only the brands you compete with, not the places that sell them.

Naming competitors is optional but makes a large difference: with names, the filter is exact; without them, you are relying on the models' own read of who competes with you. Leaving the box unticked keeps everything, which is the right choice when you are researching a category rather than planning a buy.

7. Websites, YouTube channels and apps

The three publisher types are not three flavours of the same search. Each has its own prompt, its own idea of what makes two results "the same thing", and its own results tab.

TypeThe question askedHow duplicates are matched
Websites "To answer this, which websites or publishers would you consult or cite?" By domain, ignoring https://, www. and any path.
YouTube channels "Whose content is a person with this need genuinely likely to watch?" By channel handle, so a full URL and a bare @handle collapse into one.
Applications "Which apps is a person with this need likely to have installed and use?" By app name — models return different store listings for iOS, Android and different regions, so the name is the only stable identity. Platform is kept as a detail.

Everything after that point — agreement, breadth, ranking, the final trim — is identical across the three. Each type gets its own separate ranking, so a website never competes for position with an app.

Export CSV gives you the whole ranking with all of the above for spreadsheets and decks. A practical way to read the table: treat the High-breadth rows as your core buy, the Medium rows as the expansion list, and the Low rows as leads worth a look rather than conclusions.

9. What this is not

{{ app_name }} is a research assistant, not an oracle, and it is more useful if you know exactly where its authority ends.

  • It is a prediction, not a measurement. The output reflects what these models said at the time you ran it. It is not a guarantee of citation, traffic, or that an assistant will surface a given site tomorrow.
  • It knows nothing about availability or price. No inventory, no rate cards, no reach figures. The models are instructed never to invent them, and none appear in the output.
  • Brand safety still needs a human. A publisher can be highly relevant and entirely wrong for your brand. Nothing here replaces that judgement.
  • Model knowledge has a horizon. A publisher launched very recently may be under-represented simply because the models haven't caught up.
  • Your questions bound the answer. The system can only find publishers relevant to the questions you approved. Deleting a whole line of enquiry at the review step removes everything it would have surfaced — which is precisely why that step is yours.

Used well, it is a very well-read assistant that surfaces where the conversation is happening in minutes rather than weeks, with the reasoning attached — and a human making the call.
